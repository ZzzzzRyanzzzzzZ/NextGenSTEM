Every kit arrives with its parts, a tolerance sheet, and a card listing five things other people have made with it. There is no step 1. The first hour is spent deciding, which is the part most kits quietly delete.

The argument

Instructions are a promise that someone already knows the answer.

A kit with a manual has exactly one good outcome and a hundred ways to fail at it. That is not engineering. It is following. We would rather hand over a constraint, a pile of parts and a reason to care, then get out of the way.

Four rules we hold ourselves to when we design anything here.

01

Ship the constraint, not the solution

Every kit states a limit: a budget of parts, a load to carry, a distance to cross. The limit is the assignment. What crosses the gap is the student's business.

02

Make it measurable, or don't ship it

If a build can't be compared to last week's build with a number, there is nothing to argue about, and arguing is where the learning actually happens.

03

Depth beats coverage, every time

Forty shallow topics produce recall. One topic pushed until it gets strange produces the thing we're actually after: a student who knows what a real question feels like.

04

Failure is a data point, not a defect

Parts are replaceable and cheap on purpose. A bridge that collapses at 4.2 kg told you more than one that was never loaded.

04 · The fellowship

We give the kit
away. You owe us
a build.

Price should not decide who gets to find out they are good at this. So we select a cohort by application, post them a kit for nothing, and set each one a brief in their own field.

Six weeks later they send back what they made, with the numbers and the failures attached. Outside parts are allowed. If the idea needs a servo we did not send, go and find a servo.
